Sciatica and cervical pain are not the same condition

Sciatica describes pain related to irritation of a nerve in the lower back, typically spreading into a buttock and down a leg. Cervical pain simply means neck pain. When a nerve root in the neck is irritated or compressed, it may cause cervical radiculopathy, with pain or altered sensation extending into an arm or hand. Not every neck ache or backache indicates a trapped nerve.

Sciatica: signs and symptoms

  • Pain beginning in the lower back or buttock and travelling down the back of one leg, sometimes to the foot.
  • Sharp, burning, shooting or electric-shock-like pain that may worsen with some positions or movements.
  • Tingling, pins and needles, numbness or altered sensation in the affected leg.
  • Possible leg or foot weakness.
  • Symptoms that may fluctuate and interfere with walking, sitting or daily activities.

What can cause sciatica?

Common causes include a lumbar disc herniation irritating a nerve root and age-related changes that narrow the space around nerves (spinal stenosis). Other spinal conditions can also produce radiating leg pain. Diagnosis requires assessment; symptoms alone cannot identify the exact cause.

Cervical (neck) pain: signs and symptoms

  • Neck stiffness, aching or reduced range of movement.
  • Pain around the neck, shoulders or upper back; sometimes associated headaches.
  • With cervical nerve-root irritation, pain spreading into a shoulder, arm or hand.
  • Tingling, numbness or weakness in an arm or hand when a nerve is involved.
  • Symptoms that can worsen with certain neck movements or prolonged positions.

What causes cervical pain?

Muscle strain, sustained positions and age-related changes of the cervical discs and joints can contribute to neck pain. Cervical radiculopathy may result from a disc problem or narrowing around a nerve root. Severe or progressive neurological symptoms need medical assessment.

Warning signs: when not to wait for physiotherapy

Go to emergency care immediately for new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the groin or genitals (saddle numbness), rapidly worsening leg weakness, or major neurological symptoms after spinal trauma.

Seek urgent medical assessment for new difficulty walking, reduced hand coordination or weakness alongside neck pain, or for pain with fever, unexplained weight loss, cancer history or significant recent injury. These symptoms may indicate a condition requiring tests or specialist care rather than routine exercises.

Can physiotherapy help?

For many people, physiotherapy can be part of conservative care after clinical assessment. A physiotherapist may advise on appropriate movement, individually tailored exercises, staying active and strategies for everyday tasks. Some cases need a medical review, imaging or another type of treatment instead.

Do not begin aggressive stretching, neck manipulation or unsupervised exercises if symptoms are severe, rapidly worsening or associated with neurological warning signs.

What should you tell your physiotherapist?

  • Where your pain starts and whether it travels into a leg or arm.
  • When symptoms began and what worsens or relieves them.
  • Any numbness, weakness, balance problems or previous injury.
  • Current medicines, prior spine imaging, surgery or relevant conditions.

Frequently asked questions

What are the most common symptoms of sciatica?

Sciatica typically causes pain spreading from the lower back or buttock down one leg, sometimes with tingling, numbness or weakness.

Is cervical pain the same as cervical radiculopathy?

No. Cervical pain means neck pain. Cervical radiculopathy occurs when a nerve root in the neck is irritated, causing symptoms that may spread into the shoulder, arm or hand.

Can physiotherapy help with sciatica and neck pain?

A physiotherapist may help with tailored movement, exercise, posture and activity advice after assessment. Treatment depends on the underlying cause and red flags.

When should I seek urgent medical help?

Seek emergency assessment for new bladder or bowel dysfunction, numbness around the genitals, rapidly worsening weakness, or severe neck symptoms with trouble walking or loss of hand coordination.
